# The Plain-English Report

*This document explains the project to a human reader with no coding or
statistics background. Every number in it comes from a recorded run — the
technical log is [FINDINGS.md](FINDINGS.md). A short glossary sits at the
bottom; terms it defines are marked like °this° on first use.*

## What this project is

IBM publishes a realistic *simulated* banking dataset: about 5 million
transactions between half a million accounts, where a hidden simulator played
the role of money launderers. Each transaction is labeled — laundering or
not — so a detection method can be *graded*, not just admired. About 1 in 81
accounts is touched by laundering.

The goal: given nothing but the transactions, produce a ranked worklist of
suspicious accounts, so that a human investigator's limited time lands on
real launderers instead of innocent noise.

The project's one rule, learned expensively from its predecessor: **score
first, build second.** Every idea gets graded against the labels the day it
is written, with safeguards against self-deception. Losing results are kept
and published alongside winning ones.

## What was built, in plain words

The final detector is a combination of five simple ingredients:

1. **Counting.** How many partners an account transacts with, how much money
   in and out, how many banks it touches, whether it sits in payment loops.
   Nothing clever — and it already gets you most of the way.
2. **Guilt by association, done carefully.** If an account shares
   transaction partners with *known* launderers, that's suspicious — but a
   partner shared with 14,000 other accounts (a bank's internal hub) means
   nothing. The method weights rare shared partners heavily and common ones
   barely at all.
3. **Ignore the megaphones entirely.** Fifteen counterparties in the data
   are giant institutional hubs touched by hundreds to tens of thousands of
   accounts (every other counterparty in the dataset has at most 93). Sharing
   *those* is meaningless, and before they were excluded they polluted the
   very top of the worklist — the fix for that was the project's single
   biggest practical improvement (see "The defect" below).
4. **One yes/no flag: does the account ever convert currency?** Cross-currency
   activity turned out to be a strong laundering marker in this data.
5. **A standard statistical model** (logistic regression — the "hello world"
   of machine learning) to blend the above into one score per account.

Many fancier alternatives were tried and *lost* to this: network-centrality
mathematics, category theory, weighting everything by dollar amounts (four
separate failures), two-step guilt-by-association. The pattern repeated from
the predecessor project: simple counting on honest footing beats elaborate
machinery. The catalog of everything tried is [MATH_IDEAS.md](MATH_IDEAS.md).

## What the numbers mean for a human investigator

Random guessing finds a launderer once every 81 tries. The final system:

- **Hand an investigator the top 100 accounts: about 87 are real
  launderers.** (Measured five times with reshuffled data splits; range
  83–91.)
- Top 1,000: about 59% real.
- Within the top 1,000, the accounts that convert currency — roughly 270 of
  them — are about **97% real**. That's the "start here" pile.
- Conversely, three cheap facts (never uses ACH, receives nothing, no
  self-transfers) mark accounts that are almost certainly *not* worth time.

Before the hub fix (item 3 above), the top 100 was only 4% real — the
system's most confident picks were its worst. A single summary statistic
(°AUROC°) completely hid this; only checking the actual top-100 worklist
revealed it. That lesson is now baked into every script.

## Why the numbers deserve some trust

Four safeguards run on every method, every time:

- **The shuffle test.** Rerun the method with the answer labels randomly
  scrambled. If it still "works," it's grading itself, not detecting
  anything. (Every reported method collapses to coin-flip on shuffled
  labels, as it should.)
- **Five different splits.** With few launderers, one lucky split of the
  data can flatter a method. Every headline number is the average of five.
- **Operational cuts.** Precision is reported at top-100/500/1000 — the
  sizes of real worklists — not only at statistically convenient points.
- **The transfer test.** The finished recipe was *frozen* — no adjustments
  allowed — and run on IBM's other datasets, generated by different
  simulation runs the recipe had never seen:
  - LI-Small (7M transactions, different run): still 63x better than
    guessing at the top of the list.
  - HI-Medium (32M transactions): nearly identical quality to home turf,
    and the currency-conversion pile was 99.6% pure — one split had it
    literally 435 correct out of 435.
  - HI-Large (180M transactions, 2.1M accounts): completed on a 32 GB
    laptop — though only after rewriting the bookkeeping from Python
    dictionaries into compact integer arrays; the naive version would not
    have fit. About 87 of the top 100 are real launderers there too, and
    the currency pile stays ~96% pure.

## What it cannot do — the honest limits

- **Half the crime leaves no shape.** About 50% of flagged accounts in this
  data commit single, isolated illicit transactions with no structure — no
  loops, no shared partners, no pattern. No structural method can find them,
  and this one finds almost none of them. The ~59% top-1000 precision is
  close to the ceiling *any* method of this kind can reach here.
- **Two laundering schemes are nearly invisible.** IBM's ground-truth files
  name each laundering scheme. Two of them ("stack" and "bipartite" — money
  relayed through chains or scattered across disjoint one-off pairs) touch
  each account only once or twice with fresh partners. Two targeted
  detection attempts both failed, and the failure is structural, not a bug:
  those schemes leave no per-account trace. Catching them would require
  analyzing individual transactions over time — a deliberate methodological
  door this project has so far chosen not to open, because time-based
  parameters are the easiest place to fool yourself.
- **It's a simulation.** A method that works on IBM's simulator is not
  thereby proven on real banking data. The transfer tests show it isn't
  merely memorizing one simulation run — but real-world validation would
  need real, labeled banking data, which essentially never leaves banks.

## What remains open

1. The time-dimension decision: whether to build receive-then-forward
   transaction chaining, with a justification stated *before* seeing results.
2. Why ranking quality glides gently downward as datasets grow denser
   (0.90 → 0.87 → 0.85 across the three HI sizes) — real, unexplained,
   operationally minor.
3. Real-data validation, if labeled data ever becomes available.

## Glossary

- **AUROC** — a 0-to-1 grade of ranking quality: the probability that a
  randomly chosen launderer is ranked above a randomly chosen innocent
  account. 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect. This project's best: ~0.90 on
  home data, ~0.81–0.87 on transfer data. Useful, but it summarizes the
  *whole* ranking — it can hide a rotten top-100, which is why worklist
  precision is always reported beside it.
- **Precision at top-k** — of the k highest-scored accounts, the fraction
  that are actually laundering. The number an investigator actually feels.
- **Lift** — how many times better than random guessing. 70x lift means one
  find per ~1.2 tries instead of one per 81.
- **Base rate** — the fraction of accounts that are laundering if you pick
  blindly (1.23% here).
- **Seed / split** — the random shuffle deciding which accounts are used to
  teach the model vs. test it. Five different shuffles guard against luck.
- **Frozen recipe** — running the method on new data with zero changes, so
  the new score is a genuine test rather than a re-tuning exercise.
- **Hub** — a counterparty touched by an enormous number of accounts (a
  bank's internal account, effectively). Fifteen of them exist in every IBM
  dataset tested, always the same "bank 70" family; all are excluded.
